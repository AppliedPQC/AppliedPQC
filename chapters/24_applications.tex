\chapter{Deploying Post-Quantum Cryptography}
\label{ch:applications}

\section{Why this chapter matters}
The previous parts built and analysed the algorithms. This chapter is about the harder half of the problem: getting them into web protocols, digital certificates, and infrastructure correctly and in time. The forcing function is \emph{harvest now, decrypt later} (Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum}): an adversary can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it once a quantum computer exists, so any long-lived secret is already exposed. Migration must therefore begin before the threat arrives, not after, and migration is an engineering project at least as much as a mathematical one.

\section{Learning goals}
After reading this chapter, you should be able to:

\begin{itemize}
  \item explain why real deployments use \emph{hybrid} classical-plus-postquantum cryptography, what crypto-agility means, and when hybrid is the wrong choice;
  \item describe how TLS 1.3 carries a hybrid key exchange and post-quantum certificates, and what the size costs are;
  \item evaluate how those sizes interact with packet loss, QUIC amplification limits, deployment tooling, and PKI structure;
  \item say what has already shipped, and why key exchange migrated years ahead of signatures;
  \item weigh the size and performance trade-offs that drive scheme choice in practice.
\end{itemize}

\section{Hybrid cryptography and crypto-agility}
The safest way to deploy a young algorithm is not to trust it alone. A \emph{hybrid} scheme combines a well-understood classical algorithm with a post-quantum one through an analysed protocol construction.

\begin{definition}[Hybrid key exchange]
A hybrid key exchange combines a classical key-agreement contribution (for example
X25519 ECDHE) with a post-quantum KEM contribution (for example ML-KEM-768) through
an analysed protocol construction. X25519 is Diffie--Hellman key agreement, not a
KEM. A robust construction binds algorithm identifiers, peer identities, and the
handshake transcript in a KDF extract/expand step. Its design goal is to retain
session-key security when at least one component remains secure, subject to the
combiner and protocol's required conditions; arbitrary hash concatenation is not a
general theorem.
\end{definition}

Hybrid deployment hedges two opposite risks at once: a future quantum computer breaking the classical part, and an as-yet-undiscovered classical weakness in the still-young post-quantum part. It is the mainstream migration strategy precisely because it removes the need to bet everything on one new assumption.

\subsection{The combiner, concretely}
\label{sec:kem-combiner}

The definition above says ``an analysed protocol construction'' and warns that
arbitrary hashing is not one. It is worth seeing what a construction that
\emph{is} analysed looks like, because the reason for each of its inputs is the
whole content of the warning.

\paragraph{First, X25519 is not a KEM.}
Diffie--Hellman is key \emph{agreement}, so it has to be wrapped before it will
compose with one. The standard wrapping makes the ephemeral public key play the
role of the ciphertext:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{A KEM from Diffie--Hellman (here X25519~\cite{rfc7748})}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Statex \textbf{KeyGen:}
\State $sk \gets$ random scalar; \quad $pk \gets sk\cdot G$
\Statex \textbf{Encaps}$(pk)$:
\State $r \gets$ random scalar
\State $ct \gets r\cdot G$ \Comment{the ephemeral public key \emph{is} the ciphertext}
\State $ss \gets r\cdot pk$ \Comment{$= rsk\cdot G$}
\State \Return $(ct, ss)$
\Statex \textbf{Decaps}$(sk, ct)$:
\State \Return $ss \gets sk\cdot ct$ \Comment{$= skr\cdot G$}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Then the combiner.}
With both components in KEM form, the hybrid is componentwise, and everything
interesting happens on one line.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Generic hybrid KEM from a classical $\mathsf{KEM}_1$ and a post-quantum $\mathsf{KEM}_2$}
\label{alg:hybrid-kem}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Statex \textbf{KeyGen:}
\State $(pk_1, sk_1) \gets \mathsf{KEM}_1.\mathsf{KeyGen}()$
\State $(pk_2, sk_2) \gets \mathsf{KEM}_2.\mathsf{KeyGen}()$
\State \Return $pk = pk_1 \| pk_2$, \quad $sk = sk_1 \| sk_2$
\Statex \textbf{Encaps}$(pk)$:
\State parse $pk_1 \| pk_2 \gets pk$
\State $(ct_1, ss_1) \gets \mathsf{KEM}_1.\mathsf{Encaps}(pk_1)$
\State $(ct_2, ss_2) \gets \mathsf{KEM}_2.\mathsf{Encaps}(pk_2)$
\State $ss \gets \mathrm{KDF}\big(ss_1 \,\|\, ss_2 \,\|\, ct_1 \,\|\, ct_2 \,\|\, \textit{label}\big)$
\State \Return $ct = ct_1 \| ct_2$, \quad $ss$
\Statex \textbf{Decaps}$(sk, ct)$:
\State parse $sk_1 \| sk_2 \gets sk$; \quad $ct_1 \| ct_2 \gets ct$
\State $ss_1 \gets \mathsf{KEM}_1.\mathsf{Decaps}(sk_1, ct_1)$
\State $ss_2 \gets \mathsf{KEM}_2.\mathsf{Decaps}(sk_2, ct_2)$
\State \Return $ss \gets \mathrm{KDF}\big(ss_1 \,\|\, ss_2 \,\|\, ct_1 \,\|\, ct_2 \,\|\, \textit{label}\big)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Why the ciphertexts are in the hash.}
This is the step that turns the construction into a theorem, and the reason
$\mathrm{KDF}(ss_1 \| ss_2)$ alone is not one. Suppose the ciphertexts were
omitted. An IND-CCA attacker holds a challenge $(ct_1^{*}, ct_2^{*})$ and wants
the key behind it. If it can find any $ct_1' \neq ct_1^{*}$ that decapsulates to
the \emph{same} $ss_1^{*}$, then $(ct_1', ct_2^{*})$ is a \textbf{different}
ciphertext with an \textbf{identical} shared secret. It is not the challenge, so
the decapsulation oracle will answer it --- and the answer is the challenge key.
The game is lost without either component having been broken.

Binding $ct_1 \| ct_2$ closes this: change any byte of the ciphertext and the
derived key changes with it. Giacon, Heuer, and Poettering~\cite{ghp2018} showed
that the naive concatenation combiner is not generically CCA-preserving and gave
combiners that are; Bindel, Brendel, Fischlin, Goncalves, and
Stebila~\cite{bbfgs2019} carried the analysis into the hybrid KEM and
authenticated-key-exchange setting. NIST's KEM recommendations
SP~800-227~\cite{nistsp800227} now give the corresponding standards-side guidance
on hybrid constructions and approved key combiners. The \emph{label} is domain
separation, so that a key derived here cannot collide with one derived by another
protocol from the same primitives.

\begin{remark}[Two places the binding can live]
Algorithm~\ref{alg:hybrid-kem} binds the ciphertexts inside the combiner because
it must stand alone. A protocol that already hashes its full transcript can get
the same property from the protocol instead --- which is exactly what TLS does
(Section~\ref{sec:tls-hybrid}), and why its combiner looks simpler than this one.
Neither is more correct; what matters is that the binding exists somewhere, and a
combiner lifted out of a protocol that was providing it silently loses it.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{X-Wing: a real instantiation, and a caution.}
X-Wing~\cite{xwing2024} is a standalone hybrid KEM of ML-KEM-768 with X25519,
designed for use where no protocol supplies a transcript. Its combiner is
\[
  ss \;=\; \mathrm{SHA3\text{-}256}\big(ss_{\mathrm{M}} \,\|\, ss_{\mathrm{X}} \,\|\, ct_{\mathrm{X}} \,\|\, pk_{\mathrm{X}} \,\|\, \textit{XWingLabel}\big),
\]
with a six-byte label and a $1216$-byte encapsulation key against a $1120$-byte
ciphertext. Note what is \emph{missing}: the ML-KEM ciphertext $ct_{\mathrm{M}}$
is not hashed in. That is not an oversight and not a general licence --- the
specification is explicit that it holds \emph{for the concrete choice of
ML-KEM-768}, justified by that scheme's own proven properties. The transferable
lesson is the one the definition above states abstractly: a combiner may be
shortened only with a proof about the particular schemes, never by inspection.

Hybrid deployment also presupposes crypto-agility. The lifecycle requirements
beyond algorithm negotiation are developed in
Section~\ref{sec:crypto-agility}.

\subsection{Where hybrid does not fit}
\label{sec:hybrid-limits}

Hybrid is the appropriate default for the protocols above. The reasons are
properties of those protocols rather than facts about cryptography, and one
important class of system has none of them.

In TLS, a hybrid transition is nearly free. The extra classical key exchange
costs a few hundred bytes on a connection that is discarded seconds later, both
endpoints negotiate the algorithm without asking anyone's permission, and no user
is aware the change happened. The cost is paid once, by software vendors, and it
is invisible.

On a blockchain, every one of those properties fails. The extra signature is not
transient: it is written permanently into a block, relayed to every node, and
stored by every archive, so a hybrid transaction costs its wire size
\emph{forever} and to \emph{everyone}. There is no negotiation, because consensus
rules are the same for all participants. And the change is emphatically not
invisible --- users must act, first to move to the hybrid scheme and then again
to move off it, which means the ecosystem pays the coordination cost of a
migration \textbf{twice}. Chapter~\ref{ch:blockchains} shows what that
coordination cost actually looks like; it is the dominant term.

The generalizable rule is that hybrid is cheap where the redundant work is
\emph{ephemeral, negotiated, and invisible}, and expensive where it is
\emph{persistent, consensus-fixed, and user-visible}. Before adopting hybrid by
reflex, check which of those two descriptions the system fits.

\subsection{Crypto-agility as lifecycle engineering}
\label{sec:crypto-agility}

Crypto-agility is more than negotiating an identifier. Standards evolve, new
cryptanalysis changes confidence, implementations require patches, and long-lived
devices or roots may outlast an algorithm choice. Industry guidance therefore
describes agility as the ability to discover cryptographic assets and replace or
upgrade algorithms in a controlled way without breaking the business system
\cite{keyfactoragility}.

Four capabilities make that definition operational:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{inventory}: locate algorithms, keys, certificates, HSM policies,
        libraries, protocol endpoints, and data whose confidentiality lifetime is
        relevant;
  \item \textbf{abstraction}: prevent applications from depending on one concrete
        key or object format where policy should make the choice;
  \item \textbf{policy}: control allowed algorithms, parameter sets, hybrid rules,
        expiry, and exceptions centrally enough to audit them;
  \item \textbf{automation}: rotate, test, deploy, observe, and roll back at the
        scale of the infrastructure.
\end{enumerate}

Agility is constrained by wire and consensus protocols. An API cannot negotiate
an identifier the peer does not understand, shrink a fixed certificate field, or
change a blockchain transaction format. Consequently, the migration unit is the
algorithm, implementation, protocol, and infrastructure together.

\section{Post-quantum TLS 1.3}
TLS 1.3 secures most web traffic, and it is the first place post-quantum cryptography has reached production. Two independent parts of the handshake must be upgraded: the \emph{key exchange} (confidentiality) and the \emph{authentication} (signatures on certificates).

\subsection{Hybrid key exchange}
\label{sec:tls-hybrid}
The framework is settled: RFC~9954~\cite{rfc9954} specifies how TLS 1.3 carries a
hybrid key exchange, and the concrete ECDHE--ML-KEM groups and their code points
are given by a Transport Layer Security working-group
draft~\cite{tlsecdhemlkem} --- \texttt{X25519MLKEM768} (code point $4588$,
\texttt{0x11EC}), \texttt{SecP256r1MLKEM768}, and \texttt{SecP384r1MLKEM1024}.
The current revision specifies their wire formats precisely, but it remains an
active Internet-Draft and must still be treated as work in progress.

TLS takes the simpler route flagged in Section~\ref{sec:kem-combiner}: the two
shared secrets are \emph{concatenated} and inserted in place of the ordinary
(EC)DHE input to the existing TLS~1.3 key schedule. RFC~9954 requires every
component to have a \emph{fixed-length} shared secret, which makes the
concatenation unambiguous; under that condition its analysis identifies the
construction with a dual-PRF combiner \cite{rfc9954}. HKDF-Extract~\cite{rfc5869}
forms the handshake secret from the concatenated input. Subsequent
\texttt{Derive-Secret} operations bind the handshake transcript, including the
negotiated key shares; HKDF-Extract itself does not hash that transcript.

\begin{remark}[Why the shared-secret concatenation order differs]
In \texttt{X25519MLKEM768} the concatenation is $ss_{\text{ML-KEM}} \|
ss_{\text{X25519}}$, ML-KEM first --- and the reason is regulatory rather than
cryptographic. NIST SP~800-56C approves HKDF with two shared secrets on the
condition that the \emph{first} comes from a FIPS-approved key-establishment
scheme. X25519 is not one, so ML-KEM must lead. In the two \texttt{SecP} groups
the ECDHE share \emph{is} approved, so there the order reverses and ECDHE leads.
The order satisfies the stated key-derivation compliance constraint; it is not a
ranking of the components' security.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Post-quantum authentication and its size cost}
Post-quantum certificate authentication is still a separate standardization and
deployment track. ML-DSA-65 is one plausible illustrative choice for its balance of
speed and size, while SLH-DSA offers a more conservative assumption at larger sizes.
The practical obstacle is size: post-quantum public keys and signatures are far larger
than the elliptic-curve ones they replace, and a TLS handshake may carry several
certificates. The extra kilobytes cost round-trips and can collide with buffer and
fragmentation limits.

\subsection{What has actually shipped}
\label{sec:pq-adoption}

It is easy to read a chapter like this one as describing a future. For key
exchange, it does not. Hybrid post-quantum key agreement is on by default in
mainstream browsers and in OpenSSH~\cite{opensshpq}, and the share of
human-initiated HTTPS traffic reaching Cloudflare under a post-quantum key
agreement passed roughly one half during 2025~\cite{cloudflarepq2025}. Major
cloud key-management and transport services offer post-quantum KEMs as a
supported option. Google Cloud's roadmap, published in August 2026, is
representative and carries dates: hybrid ML-KEM already serves its public API
endpoints and, on an opt-in basis, its load balancers; ML-KEM, ML-DSA and
SLH-DSA are generally available in its key-management service; and the stated
target is quantum-safe infrastructure by 2029, aligned with the 2030--2035
deprecation timelines of CNSA 2.0 and NIST IR 8547~\cite{googlecloudpqc2026}.
The same roadmap instantiates the asymmetry described next: its key-exchange
items are marked complete, while its certificate-issuance and signing items
are scheduled for 2027 and 2028. A reader who has browsed the web or opened an SSH session
recently has, with high probability, already used ML-KEM.

Authentication is the opposite story, and the contrast is the useful part.
Signature migration has barely begun: post-quantum certificates remain a
draft and experimental track, public certificate authorities are only starting
to issue them, and the chains of trust behind code signing, firmware, and secure
boot turn over on a timescale of years. The asymmetry has a simple cause.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Key exchange is \textbf{ephemeral and two-party}. Both endpoints
        negotiate afresh on every connection, so an upgrade propagates as fast as
        software updates do, and nothing needs to be re-issued.
  \item Signatures are \textbf{long-lived and multi-party}. A root certificate,
        a signed firmware image, or an on-chain public key commits a verifier who
        may not be upgraded --- or may not even exist --- yet.
\end{itemize}

That distinction recurs for the rest of this book. It is why ``harvest now,
decrypt later'' is the urgent framing for confidentiality but the wrong framing
for signatures, and it is why the hardest case in the next chapter is a
signature case.

Implementation and deployment status changes rapidly. Before making a production decision, consult the current IETF document and the release notes of the selected browser, TLS library, or service for its supported groups, defaults, and authentication behaviour.

\section{Network and infrastructure effects}

\subsection{TLS performance and network amplification}

End-to-end TLS measurements show why ``PQC is slower'' is too crude. On a clean,
high-bandwidth path, several KEMs have competitive computation. Performance can
degrade when larger objects cross records, packets, initial congestion windows, or
loss-recovery boundaries \cite{pqtlsperformance2023,pqtlspqcrypto2020}.

If an exchange occupies $N$ independently lost packets with loss probability $p$,
the probability that at least one is lost is

\[
  1-(1-p)^N.
\]

For the illustrative $N=16$, $p=0.05$ case used in early PQTLS evaluation, the
result is about $56\%$. This is not a constant of PQTLS; it shows how extra bytes
can move a connection into a qualitatively different loss regime.

Certificate authentication adds a second multiplier because signatures appear in
certificate chains and in \texttt{CertificateVerify}. KEMTLS removes the server's
online handshake signature by authenticating with a KEM while retaining the CA's
offline certificate signature \cite{kemtls2020}. It demonstrates that protocol
restructuring can outperform repeated attempts to optimize one signature object;
it is an alternative design, not the deployed default of TLS~1.3.

A reproducible PQTLS experiment should pin library and draft versions, compiler and
build flags, CPU or accelerator configuration, and certificate-chain contents. It
should control RTT, bandwidth, loss, and MTU; retain packet captures and TLS message
sizes; and record the negotiated group and signature algorithm for every run.
Otherwise a benchmark may silently measure a classical fallback or an
unrepresentative clean path.

\subsection{QUIC: when bytes alter a transport state machine}

QUIC integrates TLS~1.3 with packetization, encryption levels, loss recovery, and
address validation. Before validating a client's address, a server is limited to
roughly three times the bytes it has received. Larger client Initial packets can
increase the server's budget, but larger certificates and signatures can consume it
quickly. The resulting behaviour depends on the number and timing of QUIC packets,
not merely on the sum of TLS bytes \cite{rfc9000}.

Measurements across QUIC implementations show that post-quantum objects change
packet counts and handshake behaviour and can expose assumptions in buffering and
recovery paths \cite{quantumquic2024}. Cloud-network experiments provide further
evidence that large post-quantum signatures increase the number of handshake packets
\cite{pqquiccloud2023}. This must be distinguished from IP fragmentation: a larger
handshake creates more QUIC packets without necessarily fragmenting an IP packet.

A useful QUIC evaluation records ClientHello and server-flight bytes at every
encryption level; Initial, Handshake, and 1-RTT packet counts; anti-amplification
blocking; retransmissions and PTOs; completion time and CPU cost; and results under
several RTT, bandwidth, MTU, and loss regimes.

\subsection{Industrial delivery chains and PKI}

A standard becomes deployable only after libraries, protocol negotiation, build
configuration, conformance tests, products, and both endpoints line up. Library
support for ML-KEM or ML-DSA does not prove that a production connection negotiated
it. OpenSSL, BoringSSL/Chrome, Cloudflare, and wolfSSL illustrate different links
in this chain \cite{opensslpq2026,chromekyber2024,cloudflarepqmigration2026,wolfsslpq}.

Section~\ref{sec:pq-adoption} explains why key establishment moved first. The
remaining signature migration must coordinate keys, certificates, CAs, HSMs,
relying-party software, revocation, and audit policy; library support for one
primitive does not close that delivery-chain gap.

Migration is also an opportunity to remove inherited redundancy. RPKI inherited a
large part of the X.509 object model; adding larger post-quantum signatures without
redesign can compound certificate and encoding overhead. iRPKI proposes removing
unnecessary end-entity certificate structure, retaining the authorization and CA
signature actually required, and using a more compact encoding \cite{irpki2026}.
The broader rule is to eliminate architectural redundancy before paying to protect
it with larger objects.

\section{Implementation optimization across platforms}

Primitive timings alone do not determine whether an implementation is deployable.
On a constrained device, code size, peak stack and heap use, radio bytes, and energy
per operation matter together; on a slow link, transmitting a larger certificate
can cost more energy than the cryptographic arithmetic itself
\cite{pqciotsurvey2024}. Embedded TLS measurements likewise show that post-quantum
key establishment can be practical while certificate authentication remains the
harder step \cite{pqtlsembedded2020}. A deployment should therefore test KEM,
certificate, and online-signing capability separately instead of assigning one
undifferentiated ``PQC-ready'' label.

Typical constrained-device techniques include in-place polynomial transforms,
buffer reuse between phases, and regenerating public matrices from seeds. Hardware
accelerators instead trade look-up tables, memory ports, DSP blocks, power, and
pipeline depth; server and GPU designs add batching, queueing, data-transfer, and
tail-latency constraints. An optimization is meaningful only when its benefit
survives serialization, transmission, and the workload in which the primitive is
actually used. Section~\ref{sec:fndsa-acceleration} gives a concrete FN-DSA example.

Operations that handle secret keys,
ephemeral secrets, or secret-dependent sampling -- especially key generation, signing,
and decapsulation -- require side-channel review against the applicable threat model
(Chapter~\ref{ch:sidechannel}). Public-input verification has different confidentiality
requirements, though it still needs robustness and denial-of-service review.

\section{Pitfalls}

\paragraph{Pitfall 1: deploying post-quantum only, too early.}
Dropping the classical algorithm and trusting the post-quantum one alone forfeits the hedge that hybrid provides. Until the post-quantum schemes have had many more years of scrutiny, hybrid is the conservative default --- in the protocols where hybrid is cheap. Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-limits} gives the test for when it is not, and the mirror-image mistake, adopting hybrid where it doubles a permanent cost and forces users to migrate twice, is just as real.

\paragraph{Pitfall 2: ignoring handshake and message sizes.}
Post-quantum keys and signatures are kilobytes, not tens of bytes. Buffers, packet-size assumptions, and certificate-chain limits that were fine for elliptic curves can silently break; sizes must be designed for, not discovered in production.

\paragraph{Pitfall 3: assuming a reference implementation is deployable.}
Reference code optimizes for clarity, not for constant-time behaviour. Shipping it unmodified can reintroduce exactly the side channels of Chapter~\ref{ch:sidechannel}.

\paragraph{Pitfall 4: hardcoding one algorithm.}
The standards will keep changing. A system that cannot negotiate or swap algorithms will have to be re-engineered at the worst possible time.

\section{Summary}
The mathematics of the previous parts defeats Shor's algorithm, but that is where the abstract scheme ends and the engineering begins:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{hybrid} classical-plus-postquantum deployment, with \textbf{crypto-agility}, is the mainstream migration strategy --- where the redundant work is ephemeral, negotiated, and invisible, and not where it is permanent, consensus-fixed, and user-visible;
  \item \textbf{TLS 1.3} hybrid key exchange is under standardization and deployment; protocol and implementation versions must be checked at the time of use;
  \item post-quantum \textbf{key exchange has already shipped} at scale, while \textbf{signature} migration has barely started --- because key exchange is ephemeral and two-party, and signatures are long-lived and multi-party;
  \item end-to-end performance depends on packetization, loss recovery, transport state, certificate structure, and the complete delivery chain, not primitive timings alone;
  \item scheme choice is a trade-off among size, speed, and assumption strength, with side-channel controls scoped to the secrets and threat model of each operation.
\end{itemize}

Post-quantum cryptography is no longer a paper exercise: the standards exist, and the migration is under way. Real security will arrive only when these schemes are deployed correctly, agilely, and side-channel-safely across the protocols and systems that the world already depends on. The next chapter turns to one of the hardest cases of all -- the blockchains, where the funds themselves are secured by exactly the signatures now at risk.
