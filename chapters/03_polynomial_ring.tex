\chapter{Polynomial Rings}
\label{ch:polyring}

\section{Why this chapter matters}
The previous chapter expressed cryptographic objects as vectors and matrices over a field. This chapter changes the algebra: instead of a vector space $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ we work in a \emph{quotient polynomial ring}
\[
R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n+1),
\]
the setting of RLWE, Kyber, and Dilithium. The payoff is structural. A single element of $R_q$ carries the same information as a whole structured $n\times n$ matrix, and multiplication in $R_q$ is a form of convolution that admits a fast transform (the NTT of Chapter~\ref{ch:ntt}). To use this ring safely we need to be precise about what it is: what a ring is, what a quotient by a polynomial means, and why the particular modulus $x^n+1$ is chosen.

\section{Learning goals}
After reading this chapter, you should be able to:

\begin{itemize}
  \item state what a (commutative) ring is and recognise $\mathbb{F}_q[x]$ as one;
  \item perform Euclidean division of polynomials and explain why it makes quotient rings well defined;
  \item construct the quotient ring $\mathbb{F}_q[x]/(f)$ and count its elements;
  \item state when the quotient is a field, and why $x^n+1$ (for $n$ a power of two) is the natural modulus;
  \item describe multiplication in $R_q$ as negacyclic convolution and connect it to the NTT.
\end{itemize}

\section{Rings and fields}
A field, from Chapter~\ref{ch:linalg}, supports addition, subtraction, multiplication, \emph{and} division. A ring is the weaker structure in which division may fail.

\begin{definition}[Commutative ring]
A \emph{commutative ring with identity} is a set $R$ with operations $+$ and $\cdot$ such that $(R,+)$ is an abelian group with identity $0$, multiplication is associative and commutative with an identity $1$, and multiplication distributes over addition. A \emph{field} is the special case in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse.
\end{definition}

The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ form a ring but not a field ($2$ has no integer inverse); the integers modulo a prime, $\mathbb{F}_q$, form a field. The distinction matters here because we will build new rings out of polynomials, and those are generally not fields.

\section{The polynomial ring $\mathbb{F}_q[x]$}
\begin{definition}[Polynomial ring]
For a field $\mathbb{F}$, the \emph{polynomial ring} $\mathbb{F}[x]$ consists of all finite expressions
\[
f(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_d x^d,\qquad a_i\in\mathbb{F},
\]
with addition and multiplication of polynomials. The \emph{degree} $\deg f$ is the largest $i$ with $a_i\neq 0$. It is a commutative ring with identity $1$.
\end{definition}

A polynomial is, coefficient by coefficient, just a vector: $f(x)=3x^4+2x^2+5$ is the coefficient list $(5,0,2,0,3)$. Addition of polynomials is addition of these coefficient vectors. The new ingredient is \emph{multiplication}, and it is not entrywise.

\begin{proposition}[Multiplication is convolution]
If $f(x)=\sum_i a_i x^i$ and $g(x)=\sum_j b_j x^j$, then
\[
(fg)(x) = \sum_{k} c_k x^k, \qquad c_k = \sum_{i+j=k} a_i b_j.
\]
The coefficient sequence $(c_k)$ is the \emph{convolution} of $(a_i)$ and $(b_j)$.
\end{proposition}

For example $(x+1)(x+2)=x^2+3x+2$, where the middle coefficient $3=1\cdot 2+1\cdot 1$ is a sum of cross terms. This convolution is the operation that the NTT will later accelerate.

\subsection{Sage experiment: the polynomial ring}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
F = GF(17)
R.<x> = PolynomialRing(F)
f = 3*x^4 + 2*x^2 + 5
print(f.degree())        # 4
print(f.list())          # [5, 0, 2, 0, 3]: the coefficient vector
print((x+1)*(x+2))       # x^2 + 3*x + 2
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Euclidean division}
The one structural fact that makes everything below work is that polynomials over a field can be divided with remainder, exactly like integers.

\begin{theorem}[{Division algorithm in $\mathbb{F}[x]$}]
For $f,g\in\mathbb{F}[x]$ with $g\neq 0$ there exist \emph{unique} polynomials $q$ (quotient) and $r$ (remainder) with
\[
f = q\,g + r, \qquad \deg r < \deg g.
\]
\end{theorem}

Uniqueness of the remainder is what will let us reduce every polynomial modulo a fixed $g$ to a single canonical form of degree below $\deg g$. That canonical form is the coefficient vector we actually store.

\section{Quotient rings}
Left unbounded, degrees grow without limit under multiplication. We tame this by declaring a fixed polynomial to be zero.

\begin{definition}[Ideal and quotient ring]
The \emph{principal ideal} generated by $f\in\mathbb{F}[x]$ is the set of all multiples $(f)=\{f\cdot h : h\in\mathbb{F}[x]\}$. The \emph{quotient ring} $\mathbb{F}[x]/(f)$ has as its elements the residue classes of polynomials modulo $f$; two polynomials are identified when they differ by a multiple of $f$. Arithmetic is done as in $\mathbb{F}[x]$ and then reduced modulo $f$.
\end{definition}

By Euclidean division, every class has a unique representative of degree less than $\deg f$, so the quotient is not some abstract fog: it is a concrete, finite set of low-degree polynomials.

\begin{proposition}[Size of the quotient]
If $\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_q$ and $\deg f = n$, then $\mathbb{F}_q[x]/(f)$ has exactly $q^n$ elements, one for each coefficient vector $(c_0,\dots,c_{n-1})\in\mathbb{F}_q^n$.
\end{proposition}

So as a set, $R_q=\mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n+1)$ is just $\mathbb{F}_q^n$; what the ring structure adds on top of the vector space is a \emph{multiplication}.

\subsection{Sage experiment: building a quotient ring}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
R.<x> = PolynomialRing(GF(17))
S.<y> = R.quotient(x^4 + 1)
print(y^4)     # 16 == -1 mod 17: the relation x^4 = -1
print(y^5)     # 16*y == -y
\end{lstlisting}

\section{When is the quotient a field?}
Whether $\mathbb{F}[x]/(f)$ is a field is governed entirely by whether $f$ can be factored.

\begin{theorem}[Irreducibility and fields]
$\mathbb{F}[x]/(f)$ is a field if and only if $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{F}$. If $f$ factors non-trivially, the quotient has zero divisors and is only a ring.
\end{theorem}

This is the polynomial analogue of ``$\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ is a field iff $m$ is prime.'' It will matter twice. Over $\mathbb{Q}$ the modulus $x^n+1$ is irreducible (below), which is what gives the associated number field its clean structure; but over $\mathbb{Z}_q$ the \emph{same} polynomial deliberately factors, and that factorisation is precisely what the NTT exploits.

\section{The modulus $x^n+1$ and cyclotomic polynomials}
Modern lattice schemes fix $n$ to be a power of two and work modulo $x^n+1$. This choice is not cosmetic.

\begin{definition}[Cyclotomic polynomial]
The $m$-th \emph{cyclotomic polynomial} $\Phi_m(x)$ is the minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ of a primitive $m$-th root of unity; its roots are exactly the primitive $m$-th roots of unity.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
For $n=2^k$, one has $x^n+1 = \Phi_{2n}(x)$, and it is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.
\end{proposition}

Thus $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^n+1)$ is the ring of integers of the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{2n})$ -- a highly structured, well-studied object, which is the deeper reason it supports both strong security reductions and fast arithmetic. Working modulo a power-of-two degree also makes the reduction step trivial to implement, as the next section shows.

\subsection{Negacyclic wrap-around}
The relation $x^n=-1$ has a very concrete effect on the coefficient vector. Multiplying by $x$ shifts every coefficient one slot to the right, and the coefficient that would fall off the top wraps back into slot $0$ with its sign flipped.

Carrying the sign flip through a full product gives the \emph{negacyclic convolution}: the coefficients of $c(x)=a(x)b(x) \bmod (x^n+1)$ are
\[
c_k = \sum_{i+j=k} a_i b_j \;-\; \sum_{i+j=k+n} a_i b_j,
\]
the ordinary convolution minus the part that has wrapped past degree $n-1$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:polymul-negacyclic} is the direct schoolbook realisation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polynomial Multiplication modulo $x^n+1$}
\label{alg:polymul-negacyclic}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Coefficient arrays $a_0,\dots,a_{n-1}$ and $b_0,\dots,b_{n-1}$ of $a(x),b(x)\in R_q=\mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n+1)$
\Ensure Coefficients $c_0,\dots,c_{n-1}$ of $c(x)=a(x)b(x)\bmod(x^n+1)$
\State $c_0,\dots,c_{n-1} \gets 0,\dots,0$
\For{$i = 0,\dots,n-1$}
    \For{$j = 0,\dots,n-1$}
        \State $k \gets i+j$
        \If{$k < n$}
            \State $c_k \gets c_k + a_i b_j$ \Comment{ordinary term, degree stays below $n$}
        \Else
            \State $c_{k-n} \gets c_{k-n} - a_i b_j$ \Comment{wraps past degree $n-1$: negated, since $x^n=-1$}
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $(c_0,\dots,c_{n-1}) \bmod q$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Splitting modulo $q$: the door to the NTT}
Over $\mathbb{Q}$ the modulus is irreducible, but over $\mathbb{Z}_q$ it need not be, and we \emph{want} it to factor.

\begin{proposition}[Full splitting]
If $2n \mid q-1$, then $\mathbb{F}_q$ contains a primitive $2n$-th root of unity, $x^n+1$ splits into $n$ distinct linear factors over $\mathbb{F}_q$, and the Chinese Remainder Theorem gives a ring isomorphism
\[
\mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n+1) \;\cong\; \mathbb{F}_q \times \cdots \times \mathbb{F}_q \quad (n\text{ copies}).
\]
\end{proposition}

Under this isomorphism, multiplication of polynomials becomes coordinatewise multiplication of $n$ scalars -- an $O(n)$ operation once the map is applied. The map itself, and its inverse, are computed in $O(n\log n)$ by the NTT (Chapter~\ref{ch:ntt}); that is the entire reason the NTT exists.

\begin{remark}[Kyber's partial split]
Kyber uses $q=3329$ and $n=256$, and $q-1 = 3328 = 2^8\cdot 13$. Here $n=2^8$ divides $q-1$ but $2n=2^9$ does not, so $\mathbb{F}_q$ has a primitive $256$-th root of unity but no primitive $512$-th root. Consequently $x^{256}+1$ does not split completely; it factors into $128$ irreducible \emph{quadratics}, and Kyber's NTT maps $R_q$ to $128$ copies of $\mathbb{F}_q[x]/(\text{quadratic})$ rather than to $256$ scalars. The principle is identical -- CRT turns one multiplication into many independent small ones -- only the block size differs.
\end{remark}

\section{RLWE as structured LWE}
With the ring in hand, the transition from LWE to RLWE is a change of alphabet:
\[
\underbrace{A\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{b}}_{\text{LWE, over } \mathbb{F}_q^n}
\qquad\longrightarrow\qquad
\underbrace{a(x)\,s(x)+e(x)=b(x)}_{\text{RLWE, over } R_q}.
\]
The random matrix $A$ is replaced by structured polynomial multiplication, because one polynomial encodes an entire negacyclic linear map; the secret and error become polynomials. This can substantially reduce storage relative to an unstructured matrix, but there is no parameter-independent ``factor of $n$'' size theorem: a comparison depends on dimensions, sample counts, moduli, security targets, and encoding. The precise RLWE construction is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:rlwe}.

\end{lstlisting}

\section{Summary}
This chapter built the ring
\[
R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n+1)
\]
from the ground up: a polynomial ring $\mathbb{F}_q[x]$, made finite by quotienting out the ideal $(x^n+1)$, whose elements are length-$n$ coefficient vectors and whose multiplication is negacyclic convolution. Choosing $n$ a power of two makes the modulus a cyclotomic polynomial with excellent structure, and choosing $q$ so that the modulus splits mod $q$ opens the door to the NTT. The next chapter, \emph{Lattices}, turns from this algebraic view to the geometric one, where the hardness of the underlying problems actually lives.
